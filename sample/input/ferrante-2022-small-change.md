# Small Change in the Synoptic Parables: Lepta, Drachmas and Talents

_Chiara Ferrante, Università di Vallombra_

## Abstract

Recent debate about the denarius as a "day's wage" has focused on silver. This article turns to the smaller and larger denominations of the Synoptic parables, the lepton, the drachma and the talent, and argues that the evangelists use coin names as a graded scale of value whose rhetorical force does not depend on any single wage equivalence.

## 1. Introduction

The exchange between Marwick and Renner over Mt 20,2 has clarified what is at stake in reading coin names economically.[^1] Marwick defends the denarius as a realistic day's wage; Renner treats it as a narrative convention. Both, however, concentrate on a single denomination. I build on Renner's observation that the denarius was the coin of taxation rather than of the village market, and ask what happens if we look at the whole range of coins named in the Synoptic tradition.

## 2. The widow's lepta

In Mc 12,42 the widow puts in "two lepta, which make a quadrans" (λεπτὰ δύο, ὅ ἐστιν κοδράντης). The gloss converts a Greek bronze denomination into a Roman one for the benefit of the audience, and it presupposes hearers to whom the quadrans, the smallest Roman coin, was familiar. The scene works by contrast with the large gifts of the rich (Mc 12,41), not by reference to any wage.

## 3. The lost drachma

The woman of Lc 15,8-10 searches her house for one of ten drachmas. Whether or not a drachma was a day's pay, the point of the parable is the proportion of loss: one coin in ten. On the circulation of small silver in Galilean villages, W. Holt's hoard study remains fundamental, and it shows that drachmas and denarii were rare finds outside the towns.[^2] The parable's drachmas are therefore a household reserve, perhaps a dowry, rather than wages.

## 4. Talents and the scale of value

At the other end of the scale, the talents of Mt 25,14-30 and the ten thousand talents of Mt 18,24 are sums no labourer could earn. Pliny's account of the introduction of silver coinage at Rome (Plin. nat. 33,44) shows how far ancient writers themselves thought of denominations as a hierarchy whose relations mattered more than any single coin's purchasing power. The evangelists use the same graded scale: lepton, quadrans, drachma, denarius, talent.

<!-- page 212 -->

## 5. Conclusion

The coins of the Synoptic parables form a graded scale of value from the lepton to the talent. Their rhetorical force does not depend on the denarius being a day's wage, and the debate between Marwick and Renner can be partly set aside once the whole scale is in view. Where a wage is in question, as in Mt 20,2, I side with Renner, although Holt's reconstruction of household budgets suggests that Marwick's figure is not implausible as an upper bound.[^3]
