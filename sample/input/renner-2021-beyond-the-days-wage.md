# Beyond the "Day's Wage": Denarii, Rations and the Problem of Equivalence

_Tobias Renner, Universität Kesselberg_

## Abstract

The claim that a denarius was "a day's wage" rests on a narrow and largely literary base. Re-reading Mt 20,2 alongside military pay scales, ration evidence and the circulation of coin in Galilee, this article argues that the sum in the parable is a narrative convention, and that wages in the rural economy were commonly paid in kind rather than in silver.

## 1. The consensus and its sources

Pace Marwick, the denarius in Mt 20,2 is a narrative convention, not a wage datum.[^1] Her recent defence of the "day's wage" reading is the most careful statement of the consensus, but it shares the consensus's central weakness: almost all of its evidence is literary, and much of it is drawn from texts whose interest in the sum is rhetorical. The two hundred denarii of Mk 6,37 and the famine price of Offb 6,6 tell us that a denarius was a meaningful sum to a Greek-speaking audience; they do not tell us what labourers in Galilee were paid.

Tob 5,15 is often cited as an independent witness, but the drachma promised to the disguised angel is part of a romance in which every price is exemplary, and the text is in any case several centuries earlier than the gospels.[^2] The parable of the lost coin (Lk 15,8) shows a household for which a single drachma is worth a search by lamplight; it is not evidence for wages at all.

## 2. Military pay as a control

Military pay offers the only well-documented scale. Polybius gives the Roman infantryman two obols a day (Polyb. 6.39.12), from which deductions were made for food and equipment. Tacitus has the mutineers of AD 14 complain of ten asses a day (Tac. Ann. 1.17), and the complaint implies that the figure was regarded as low even for men whose rations were supplied. If soldiers in the early principate received less than a denarius a day before deductions, a civilian labourer's receiving a full denarius in silver is not the obvious default.[^3]

## 3. Wages in kind

The rabbinic regulations in m. B. Meṣ. 7:1 are better evidence than they are usually taken to be, but they point away from the cash model: the obligation to feed hired labourers "according to the custom of the province" shows that a substantial part of the wage was paid in food. An unpublished ostracon from Sepphoris (O.Sepph. 4), recording payments to field hands partly in grain and partly in bronze, fits this picture.[^4]

<!-- page 35 -->

## 4. Coin in circulation

Hoard evidence from Lower Galilee suggests that silver denarii were scarce in rural circulation before the second century, and that most everyday transactions used bronze.[^5] The tribute coin of Mk 12,15 is telling here: Jesus asks for a denarius to be brought, implying that it was not ready to hand. The denarius was the coin of imperial taxation, not of the village market.

## 5. Conclusion

The denarius of Mt 20,2 is a narrative convention rather than an attested wage. The parable uses a round silver sum to make the equal payment of the workers vivid; it should not be used as a datum for reconstructing rural incomes.
