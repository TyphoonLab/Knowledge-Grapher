# A Denarius a Day: Wage Rhetoric and Subsistence in Matthew 20:1–16

_Helena Marwick, University of Northbridge_

## Abstract

The parable of the labourers in the vineyard (Matt 20:1–16) presupposes that a single denarius is a recognisable day's pay. This article argues that the figure is not merely narrative convention but reflects the subsistence wage of an unskilled labourer in early Roman Galilee, and that the comparative evidence from Tobit, Tacitus and the Mishnah supports this reading.

## 1. Introduction

The landowner of Matthew's parable "agreed with the labourers for a denarius for the day" (Matt 20:2; συμφωνήσας δὲ μετὰ τῶν ἐργατῶν ἐκ δηναρίου τὴν ἡμέραν). Commentators have long treated this sum as the standard daily wage, and the assumption has passed into handbooks of New Testament economics with little scrutiny.[^1] The aim of this article is to test that assumption against the comparative evidence and to show that it holds, with qualifications, for the rural economy of early Roman Galilee.

The question matters beyond the parable. If a denarius was a day's wage, then the two hundred denarii of Mark 6:37 represent the earnings of a labourer for most of a year, and the disciples' objection becomes a statement about the scale of the crowd rather than an arbitrary large number.[^2] Likewise the hundred denarii owed by the fellow servant in Matt 18:28 would be a debt of some three months' labour, against the ten thousand talents of Matt 18:24.

## 2. The Denarius as Day Wage

I argue that the denarius was a day's wage for an unskilled labourer, and that the parable's audience would have heard the sum as fair but minimal. Three considerations support this.

First, the parable itself treats the denarius as the just wage: the landowner promises the later workers "whatever is right" (Matt 20:4) and then pays every worker the same denarius. The rhetoric depends on the sum being both adequate and unremarkable.

Second, the comparative evidence points to a similar level. In Tob 5:15 Tobit promises the angel Raphael, disguised as a hired guide, "a drachma a day" (δραχμὴν τῆς ἡμέρας), and in this period the drachma and the denarius were treated as roughly equivalent units of account.[^3] Tacitus reports the mutineers of AD 14 complaining that their lives were valued at ten asses a day (Tac. Ann. 1.17), a figure below the denarius and presented as degrading.

Third, the rabbinic regulations on hired labour in m. B. Meṣ. 7:1 assume that the terms of a day labourer's hire, including food, are fixed by local custom rather than by the employer's generosity. The Hebrew term for the coin, דינר, appears in such contexts as the unit in which labour is reckoned.[^4]

## 3. Subsistence and the Household

A day's wage is not the same as a household income. Following the reconstruction of rural budgets by Lisbeth Holt, I take a peasant household of five to have required roughly two hundred and fifty denarii a year for grain, oil and clothing.[^5] On that figure a labourer hired for a denarius on most working days could support a household only at the margin of subsistence, which is precisely the situation the parable presupposes when the late-hired workers stand idle in the marketplace.

The famine-price of Rev 6:6, "a quart of wheat for a denarius" (χοῖνιξ σίτου δηναρίου), confirms the point from the other direction: the visionary horror lies in the fact that a full day's wage buys only one person's daily grain.

<!-- page 104 -->

## 4. Conclusion

The denarius of Matt 20:2 should be read as a realistic subsistence wage for an unskilled labourer in early Roman Galilee. The parable's theological point about the landowner's generosity depends on this economic realism, and the comparative evidence from Tobit, Tacitus and the Mishnah supports it.
